## Section 1: Predictive Maintenance Fundamentals

### 1.1 Overview
Predictive maintenance (PdM) uses sensor data and statistical models to anticipate equipment failures
before they occur. Unlike preventive maintenance (time-based) or reactive maintenance (after failure),
PdM targets the actual condition of equipment.

Key objectives:
- Reduce unplanned downtime by detecting anomalies early
- Extend equipment life through timely intervention
- Optimise maintenance schedules based on actual condition

### 1.2 Key Sensor Parameters

**Air Temperature [K]**
Normal operating range: 295–305 K (approximately 22–32°C).
Elevated air temperature can indicate inadequate cooling, blocked ventilation, or high ambient heat load.
Sustained readings above 308 K warrant inspection of the cooling system.

**Process Temperature [K]**
Normal operating range: 304–315 K.
Process temperature typically runs 8–10 K higher than air temperature due to heat generated by the
machining process. A narrowing differential may indicate abnormal process conditions.

**Rotational Speed [rpm]**
Normal range: 1200–2800 rpm.
Speed outside this range may indicate drive issues, belt slippage, or control system faults.
Sudden drops in speed under constant load may indicate mechanical resistance (e.g., tool engagement issues).

**Torque [Nm]**
Normal range: 20–65 Nm.
High torque values (>70 Nm) under low-speed conditions can indicate mechanical binding, tooling problems,
or material hardness variations. Power = Torque × (2π × RPM / 60); excessive power can cause thermal failure.

**Tool Wear [min]**
Tool wear accumulates over time. Tools should be inspected:
- At 150 minutes of use for initial checks
- At 200 minutes for detailed assessment
- Mandatory replacement threshold: 250 minutes

Worn tools increase cutting forces (torque), generate more heat, reduce surface quality, and raise failure risk.

## Section 2: Failure Modes

### 2.1 Tool Wear Failure (TWF)
**Description:** Tool fails due to excessive wear beyond the replacement threshold.
**Indicators:** Increasing torque trend, surface quality degradation, unusual vibration or noise.
**Prevention:** Adhere to tool change schedules. Monitor torque trends; a rising torque baseline at
constant speed indicates accelerating wear.
**Immediate action:** Stop production, replace tooling, inspect machined parts for quality compliance.

### 2.2 Heat Dissipation Failure (HDF)
**Description:** Cooling system cannot remove heat generated during high-power cutting.
**Indicators:** Process temperature rising above 315 K, air temperature elevated, insufficient coolant flow.
**Prevention:** Ensure coolant system is functioning. Inspect coolant lines and nozzles.
Check temperature sensors for calibration drift.
**Immediate action:** Reduce cutting parameters, restore coolant flow, allow machine to cool before resuming.

### 2.3 Power Failure (PWF)
**Description:** Power delivered to the spindle exceeds safe operational limits.
**Formula:** P = Torque × (2π × RPM / 60). Failure risk increases when P > 9000 W.
**Indicators:** High torque at high RPM simultaneously, tripped breakers, power supply alarms.
**Prevention:** Optimise cutting parameters to keep power within rated limits.
**Immediate action:** Stop immediately. Inspect spindle drive, check electrical supply, verify tooling.

### 2.4 Overstrain Failure (OSF)
**Description:** Mechanical overload due to product of tool wear and torque exceeding structural limits.
**Indicators:** Abnormal vibration, loud mechanical noise, structural flex.
**Prevention:** Do not operate with worn tools at high torque. Replace tools proactively.
**Immediate action:** Emergency stop. Inspect structural components, drive train, and bearings.

### 2.5 Random/Unclassified Failures (RNF)
**Description:** Failures due to causes not captured by the other modes (e.g., electrical faults,
material defects, operator error).
**Rate:** Approximately 0.1% of operations.
**Prevention:** General equipment health monitoring, operator training, quality audits.

## Section 3: Inspection Procedures

### 3.1 Daily Inspection Checklist
- [ ] Visual inspection of tooling for obvious wear or chipping
- [ ] Verify coolant level and flow rate
- [ ] Check lubrication points
- [ ] Record baseline temperature and RPM readings at startup
- [ ] Review any alarms or anomaly flags from the previous shift

### 3.2 Weekly Inspection Checklist
- [ ] Measure and record tool wear using calibrated gauge
- [ ] Inspect drive belts and pulleys for wear
- [ ] Clean coolant filter and check coolant concentration
- [ ] Test emergency stop functionality
- [ ] Review prediction system outputs from the week; note recurring anomaly patterns

### 3.3 Monthly Inspection Checklist
- [ ] Full bearing inspection and lubrication
- [ ] Calibrate temperature sensors against reference
- [ ] Inspect spindle for runout and axial play
- [ ] Comprehensive electrical inspection (wiring, grounding, connections)
- [ ] Review maintenance records and adjust PM schedule if needed

## Section 4: Recommended Actions by Risk Level

### 4.1 LOW Risk (probability < 20%)
- Continue operation under standard monitoring
- Ensure next scheduled inspection is on track
- Log sensor readings for trend analysis

### 4.2 MEDIUM Risk (probability 20–50%)
- Increase monitoring frequency
- Inspect tooling at next opportunity
- Verify coolant system is operating correctly
- Review recent torque and temperature trends

### 4.3 HIGH Risk (probability > 50%)
- Plan a machine inspection at the earliest safe opportunity
- Do not schedule additional tool wear-intensive jobs until inspection is complete
- Notify maintenance supervisor
- Document sensor readings and prediction output

### 4.4 ANOMALY DETECTED
An anomaly in sensor readings does not automatically indicate failure.
- Compare current readings to normal operating baselines
- Check for sensor drift or calibration issues before escalating
- If multiple anomaly flags occur in sequence, escalate to HIGH RISK procedures

## Section 5: Model Interpretation Guidance

### 5.1 Understanding the ML Prediction
The IndustrialAI platform uses a machine learning model trained on the UCI AI4I 2020 synthetic dataset.
Key caveats:
- The model provides a PROBABILITY ESTIMATE, not a certainty of failure
- It was trained on synthetic data; real-world performance may differ
- False positives (predicted failure when none occurs) and false negatives (missed failures) are both possible
- Model predictions should be ONE INPUT among several in a maintenance decision

### 5.2 SHAP Feature Contributions
The platform provides SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values for each prediction:
- Positive SHAP values indicate the feature INCREASED the predicted failure probability
- Negative SHAP values indicate the feature DECREASED the predicted failure probability
- SHAP values explain model behaviour, NOT physical causation
- A high SHAP value for "Torque" means the model weighted that reading heavily; it does not prove
  that torque alone CAUSED the predicted failure outcome

### 5.3 Anomaly Detection vs. Failure Prediction
These are two separate signals:
- **Failure Prediction**: Binary classification — is this record likely to be a failure?
- **Anomaly Detection**: Isolation Forest — do these readings deviate from normal operating patterns?

Both can independently be true, false, or discordant. Always consider both together.

## Section 6: Limitations and Responsible Use

- The platform is a DECISION SUPPORT tool, not an autonomous control system
- Human engineering judgment must always be applied before acting on predictions
- Do not modify machine parameters based solely on model output
- The synthetic training dataset does not capture all real-world failure modes
- Retrain or recalibrate the model if equipment is significantly modified or operating conditions change
- Contact a qualified maintenance engineer for complex or safety-critical decisions
